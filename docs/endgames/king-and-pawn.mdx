# King & Pawn Endgames

King and pawn endgames are the foundation of all endgame play — the rules here echo
through every other ending. This page goes beyond the fundamentals on the
[Endgame Basics](/docs/chess-rules/endgame-basics) page (the opposition, the rule of
the square) into two decisive ideas: **key squares** and **triangulation**.

## Key Squares

Every pawn has a set of **key squares**. If your king reaches one of them, the pawn
promotes — no matter whose move it is. For a pawn on the 5th rank, the key squares
are the three squares two ranks ahead (for the `e5` pawn below: **d7, e7, f7**).

<InteractiveBoard fen="4k3/8/8/3KP3/8/8/8/8" turn="w" />

The whole endgame becomes a race: **get your king to a key square**, and you win.
The defender's job is to stop your king from ever reaching one.

## Triangulation

Sometimes you have a winning position but only if it were the *opponent's* move —
this is **zugzwang**. In the position below it's Black to move, and Black loses:
every king move gives ground, and White breaks through.

<InteractiveBoard fen="4k3/8/8/4K3/4P3/8/8/8" turn="b" />

If the same position arose with **White** to move, it would only be a draw. So White
wants to reach this exact position but with Black on the move. The king does it by
**triangulation** — taking a three-square "triangle" route to lose a single tempo,
returning to the same square having used one extra move. That hands the zugzwang back
to the opponent.

## Key Ideas

- **Aim for the key squares**, not just for pushing the pawn — the king leads.
- **The opposition is a tool**, not the goal: use it to reach a key square.
- **Triangulate to lose a move** when you need the opponent to be the one in
  zugzwang.

:::tip
Master king and pawn endgames first. Because every piece can be traded off, almost
every endgame can simplify into one — and knowing who wins the resulting K+P race
tells you what to aim for the whole game.
:::
