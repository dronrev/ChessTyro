# Basic Checkmates

Knowing how to convert an advantage into checkmate is an essential skill. These are
the fundamental mating patterns every player should be able to deliver against a
lone king. The key idea is always the same: use your pieces to **take away escape
squares** and drive the enemy king to the edge of the board.

## Queen & King vs. King

The queen does most of the work, but she needs the **king's support** to deliver
mate - otherwise the lone king could simply capture her.

The black king on a8 is checkmated: the queen on b7 covers every escape square, and
the white king on b6 defends the queen so she can't be taken:

<InteractiveBoard fen="k7/1Q6/1K6/8/8/8/8/8" turn="b" />

:::caution
When mating with a queen, watch out for **stalemate**! If the enemy king has no
legal move but is *not* in check, the game is a draw. Keep the king in check or
leave it a square until your own king arrives.
:::

## Rook & King vs. King

Without a queen it takes longer, but the method is the same. The rook cuts off a
rank or file while your king marches up to push the enemy king to the edge.

The black king on a8 is mated: the rook on h8 controls the whole 8th rank, and the
white king on a6 guards a7 and b7:

## Two Rooks vs. King

Two rooks can checkmate on their own - your king isn't even needed. This is called
the **ladder** (or "lawnmower") mate: the rooks work together, one holding the king
on a line while the other checks from the next one.

Here the rook on a8 delivers check along the 8th rank while the rook on b7 seals off
the 7th rank, leaving the black king no escape:

<InteractiveBoard fen="R3k3/1R6/8/8/8/8/8/7K" turn="b" />

:::tip
The pattern to remember: **drive the king to the edge**, then deliver mate. A lone
king can always be checkmated by K+Q or K+R, but two knights alone *cannot* force
mate.
:::
